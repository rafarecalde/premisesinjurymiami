Hurt Using Valet Parking at a Miami Hotel, Restaurant, or Condo

By Rafael Recalde, Esq. • Recalde Law Firm, P.A. • October 29, 2026

Valet at a Miami hotel, restaurant, or condominium looks like a convenience. Legally it is a handoff zone where several companies meet: the property owner, the hotel or restaurant that hired the service, the valet operator, and sometimes a separate garage company. Guests are hurt when they trip at the stand, when they are sent to walk a dark garage, and when a car the valet is driving strikes them in the lane.

A Fall at the Stand

The stand is often a podium, a stanchion, and a curb that was not designed as a waiting room. Broken curb faces, slick pavers, a step down from the lobby with no contrast, and lighting aimed at the sign rather than the ground are premises conditions. They belong to whoever maintained that entrance. That may be the hotel or the condominium, not the valet company. The hotel guide and the parking garage guide cover the building side of the same entrance.

If the valet attendant tells you to cross the drive and retrieve something from the garage yourself, the path you were sent on is part of the facts. A dark ramp or a missing pedestrian marking on that path is the same kind of condition as any other garage injury.

A Car in the Handoff Lane

If a valet driver strikes you, the auto claim against the driver and the valet company's auto coverage is the first place to look. A premises claim can exist alongside it when the stand was placed where drivers and guests could not see each other, or when the lane had no separation between people and moving cars. Those claims use different policies. Report both the crash and the layout. A police report and the property's incident report are not substitutes for each other.

Rideshare pickup zones have the same geometry without a podium. They are covered in the pickup-zone guide. Do not assume the valet company and the rideshare company are the same kind of defendant. One may employ the driver. The other may only have sent a car to a curb it does not own.

Tickets and Signs

The stub you receive, and the sign at the podium, often say the operator is not responsible for loss or damage, sometimes including injury. That language does not automatically decide a claim about a broken curb, an unlit walk, or a car driven into a group of guests. Keep the ticket. Have it read against what happened, including whether the wording is broad enough, clear enough, and the kind of risk the law will allow a business to disclaim. A short sentence on a claim check is not the whole case.

Read the uniform and the building separately. The logo on the attendant's shirt and the name on the awning are frequently unrelated companies, with unrelated insurance. Write down both before the shift changes.

What to Keep

  • The valet ticket and any text message that told you where to stand.
  • Photos of the podium, the curb, the lighting, and the path you walked.
  • Names or plate numbers if a car was involved, and the police report number.
  • The shoes, and same-day medical care.

Negligence claims arising after March 24, 2023, generally have two years under section 95.11. A government-owned garage, if that is where the valet sent you, can add section 768.28. Comparative fault under section 768.81 includes whether you stepped into a lane that was open to cars. If the only place to stand was in that lane, the photos should show that.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q: If a valet driver hits me, is that a premises case?

It may be an auto claim against the driver and the valet company's auto coverage. It may also be a premises claim if the stand was placed where drivers and guests could not see each other, or if the lane had no separation. Both can exist. They use different insurance.

Q: Does the language on the valet ticket end the claim?

A ticket or a sign may try to limit responsibility. That language does not automatically decide a claim about a broken curb, a dark handoff lane, or a car driven into a crowd of guests. Keep the ticket and have it read against what actually happened.

Q: Who else may be responsible besides the valet company?

The hotel, restaurant, or condominium that hired the valet, the garage operator, and the property owner. The uniform logo and the name on the building are often different companies.
