Visitor Falls at Miami Hospitals and Clinics

By Rafael Recalde, Esq. • Recalde Law Firm, P.A. • October 29, 2026

Families spend hours in Miami hospitals and clinics as visitors: hallways, cafeterias, bathrooms, sidewalks, and parking garages. A fall in those spaces is a premises claim about the building. It is not, by itself, a claim that the hospital committed medical malpractice. Mixing the two labels creates the wrong deadline and the wrong pre-suit steps.

A Visitor Is Not a Patient Chart

If you were not being treated, and you slipped on a freshly mopped corridor, tripped on a cord or a mat, or fell in the garage, the duty is the duty owed to a person invited onto the property. Reasonable care includes the floors, the warnings, and the maintenance of common areas. A transitory substance in a hospital cafeteria or lobby is still a business-establishment spill under section 768.0755. A permanent lip between floor materials, or a garage ramp, is a condition of the premises, closer to the issues in the slip and fall guide and the garage guide.

A claim about the medical care a patient received is a different case. It has its own pre-suit requirements and its own limitations period. Do not assume a visitor fall and a treatment claim share a calendar. If both seem possible — you were a patient who also fell because of a building condition — say so plainly so the two are sorted at the start.

Public and Private Facilities

Jackson Health System and other government hospitals and clinics can be section 768.28 defendants. That statute requires written notice, imposes a waiting period, and caps damages. The notice-of-claim guide is the checklist. A private hospital, a specialty clinic, or an urgent-care office in a shopping center is generally a private defendant, sometimes alongside the landlord who controls the lot. The name on the building and the name on the lease can differ, just as they do in other commercial property cases.

What Visitors Should Do That Day

  • Report the fall to security so an incident number exists. Ask for a copy or photograph the form.
  • Photograph the floor, the cone if one appeared only afterward, and the wider hallway so the missing sign is visible.
  • Write down the building, the floor, and the nearest elevator bank. Hospital campuses have more than one tower.
  • Keep the shoes.
  • Get examined. A hospital will sometimes offer to "check you in the emergency department." That record is useful. It is also a clinical chart. You can accept care and still decline to speculate, on a recording, about whether you were careless.

Risk management is the property insurer's voice inside the building. Courtesy from that office is not a reason to give a recorded statement about fault, your history, or how fast you were walking.

Private negligence claims arising after March 24, 2023, generally have two years under section 95.11. Government facilities add the notice rules in section 768.28, and the footage in a hallway can be overwritten long before either date. Comparative fault under section 768.81 remains available to the defense, including an argument that an obvious wet-floor sign was posted where you could see it. Photograph where the sign actually stood.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q: Is a visitor's fall medical malpractice?

Usually no. A family member who slips on a freshly mopped hallway, trips on a cord, or falls in the garage is bringing a premises claim about the building. A claim about medical care delivered to a patient is a different kind of case with its own pre-suit rules. The two should not be assumed to share a deadline.

Q: Do public hospitals follow different rules?

They can. A county hospital or other government provider may be a section 768.28 defendant, which means written notice, a waiting period, and damages caps. A private hospital or an urgent-care clinic is generally a private premises defendant.

Q: Should I give a statement to the hospital's risk office?

Treat it the way you would treat any property insurer. You can report the fall so there is a record. A recorded statement about fault, your shoes, or your medical history can be used later to reduce the claim. Get the incident report number and photograph the floor.
