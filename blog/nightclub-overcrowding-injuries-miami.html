Hurt in a Crowded Miami Nightclub?

By Rafael Recalde, Esq. • Recalde Law Firm, P.A. • October 29, 2026

South Beach, Wynwood, and Brickell sell crowded rooms. The injuries that follow are not all the same claim. A person can be crushed against a rail, knocked down a step when a crowd surges, slip on a drink that has coated the floor, or be assaulted in a room where exits and staff were already overwhelmed. The club's duty is the duty of a business that invited the public in: reasonable care for the premises, including how many people are inside and whether they can get out.

Overcrowding Is a Condition of the Property

Fire and building rules set how many people a room can hold and require exits to remain usable. A venue that blocks an exit with a speaker, chains a door, turns the house lights down past the point where steps are visible, or continues to sell entry after the room cannot move is creating a dangerous condition. You do not need a published headcount to describe what you saw. Video from a phone, a photo of an exit sign behind a crowd, and witnesses who were turned away at the door are evidence of the condition that night.

Staffing is part of the same question. If the only employees on the floor were checking identification at the street, nobody was watching the interior stairs. Prior incidents, 911 calls, and complaints about the same room are how foreseeability is shown. That is ordinary negligent-security analysis, discussed in the negligent security guide. It is not the apartment presumption.

The Apartment Security Presumption Does Not Apply Here

Section 768.0706, Florida Statutes, gives certain multifamily residential properties a presumption against liability for third-party crimes if listed measures were substantially implemented. A nightclub is not that kind of property. Do not let a club's lawyer talk about that statute as if a camera at the door ends the case.

If the injury was a crime by another patron, section 768.0701 requires the jury in a claim against the owner or operator to consider the fault of everyone who contributed, including the person who committed the crime. The criminal case against that person is a separate proceeding. It does not pause the civil deadline.

Alcohol Service Is a Narrow Statute

Friends often assume the club is liable because it kept serving drinks. Section 768.125 generally does not impose liability for serving an adult. The two exceptions are narrow: willful and unlawful sale to someone under 21, and knowingly serving a person habitually addicted to alcohol. A premises claim about the floor, the exits, or security is the claim that matches most overcrowding injuries. It should not be pleaded as if it were a dram-shop case.

A drink on a dance floor or on the tile at a service well is a transitory substance. Section 768.0755 requires proof the business knew or should have known about it. In a nightclub, that proof is often how long the floor was wet, whether servers crossed it, and whether anyone was assigned to walk the room. A dark floor with no inspection is a facts problem, not a reason to skip the statute.

Your own drinking will be an issue. Section 768.81 bars recovery if you are found more than 50 percent at fault. Intoxication is something the defense will measure. It does not automatically end a claim about a blocked exit or a fight the club let build for an hour.

What Disappears by Noon

  • Interior cameras, which many venues overwrite on a short cycle.
  • The guest list, wristband color, and the receipt that shows when you entered.
  • Names of friends who left for other clubs and will not remember the layout later.
  • The condition of the floor, which will be mopped before the next night.
  • Staff names. Uniforms do not identify who was working the stair that night.

Get medical care even if you intend to "sleep it off." A delay becomes an argument that the injury came from somewhere else. For claims arising after March 24, 2023, the general negligence deadline is two years under section 95.11. The practical deadline for the video is days.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q: Does section 768.0706's security presumption apply to nightclubs?

No. Section 768.0706, Florida Statutes, is a presumption for certain multifamily residential properties. A nightclub claim is analyzed under ordinary premises and negligent-security principles, and section 768.0701 can require a jury to weigh the fault of a person who committed a crime on the property.

Q: Is the club liable because it served alcohol?

Not automatically. Section 768.125 generally does not impose liability for serving alcohol to an adult. It has two narrow exceptions. A spill, a blocked exit, or a failure to manage a crowd is a different claim from a dram-shop claim.

Q: Can my own drinking affect the claim?

It can. Section 768.81 bars recovery when the injured person is more than 50 percent at fault. Intoxication is a fact the defense will raise. It does not, by itself, end every claim.
